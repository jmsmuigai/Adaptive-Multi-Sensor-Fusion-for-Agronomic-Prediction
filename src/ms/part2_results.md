**3.0 Results and Discussion**

**3.1 Effect of validation design**

The validation design changed the apparent skill of every model more than the choice of model did (Table 3, Figure 3). Averaged over the thirteen properties, the median model's pooled R² was [[median_model_random:.3f]] under random splitting and [[median_model_cluster:.3f]] under leave-cluster-out. It fell to [[median_model_site:.3f]] when whole sites were withheld, [[median_model_aez:.3f]] for a withheld climatic zone and [[median_model_country:.3f]] for a withheld country. Results for every model, property and design are given in Table S3. Relative to random splitting, these are losses of [[drop_site_pct:.0f]]%, [[drop_aez_pct:.0f]]% and [[drop_country_pct:.0f]]% of the apparent skill. Random splitting and leave-cluster-out gave similar values because clusters within a site share soils and land use, so a model tested on a new cluster has usually seen its neighbours. Accuracy figures obtained from random or within-site splits therefore describe interpolation within surveyed landscapes and should not be quoted as the expected accuracy in a new area.

Table 4 and Figure 4 give the leave-site-out results by property. Gaussian process regression (GPR) had the best mean rank under leave-site-out ([[rank1_site_v:.2f]]), leave-AEZ-out ([[rank1_aez_v:.2f]]) and leave-country-out ([[rank1_country_v:.2f]]), and the highest mean R² in all three ([[best_site_r2:.3f]], [[best_aez_r2:.3f]] and [[best_country_r2:.3f]]). Deep architectures were therefore not inherently disadvantaged at this sample size; the hybrid's particular design was. The next two sections show why.

**3.3 Ablation of the hybrid architecture**

Table 5 and Figure 6 report the effect of replacing each component in turn. Two components were essential. Replacing attention pooling with mean pooling reduced mean R² by [[abl_pool_mean_abs:.3f]] and harmed every property. Removing the positional embedding reduced it by [[abl_no_pos_abs:.3f]], with significant losses for [[abl_no_pos_nsigneg]] properties. Both results have the same cause. Without positional information, pooling over spectral positions discards where in the spectrum a feature occurred, and that location is what distinguishes, for example, a carbonate band from a clay hydroxyl band. Attention pooling with a positional embedding can recover some of this information by weighting positions; without the embedding, attention pooling loses most of its value. Any attention-pooled spectral model therefore needs explicit positional information.

Table 5: Ablation of the hybrid under leave-site-out. Each row replaces one component of the full model, keeping the selected configuration fixed (three seeds; five for the linear head). ΔR² is the change in pooled R² relative to the full model, averaged over the thirteen properties; significant gains or losses are properties whose paired 95% group-bootstrap interval for ΔR² excludes zero.

[[TABLE:T5]]

![](FIG6)

Fig. 6. Ablation of the hybrid under leave-site-out. Bars give the mean change in pooled R² across the thirteen properties when one component is replaced; grey points are individual properties.

None of the components that motivated the hybrid design improved prediction. Replacing the RBF head with a linear output layer raised mean R² by [[abl_head_linear:.3f]] (better for [[abl_head_linear_nb]] of 13 properties), and a dense ReLU head gave [[abl_head_mlp:+.3f]]. A fixed-centre Gaussian-activation head was slightly worse than the learned-centre RBF head ([[abl_head_gauss:+.3f]]), with no significant difference for any property. Replacing the modality gate with plain concatenation changed R² by [[abl_fuse_concat:+.3f]]. Using attention to re-weight positions before flattening, rather than to pool them, changed R² by [[abl_pool_reweight:+.3f]], so the remaining gap to the plain CNN is not explained by any single module. Removing the ancillary input lowered R² by [[abl_spectra_only_abs:.3f]], with significant losses for [[abl_spectra_only_nsigneg]] properties, so sampling depth carries real information. Adding latitude and longitude did not help on average ([[abl_plus_coords:+.3f]]): it helped three properties and harmed three, as expected when coordinates are used to extrapolate into unsampled space (Meyer and Pebesma, 2021). Adding coordinates to LightGBM likewise gave no average benefit (mean ΔR² below 0.001 in magnitude).

In short, the hybrid works only to the extent that it behaves like a CNN with position-aware pooling, and its distinctive components (the RBF head and the gate) add parameters and seed instability without adding skill. The architecture therefore does not achieve its design objective. What the experiment does show is narrower: a differentiable RBF head *can* be trained jointly with a convolutional encoder. That is already well established (van Amersfoort *et al.*, 2020; Wilson *et al.*, 2016), and it is not, on this evidence, a reason to use one.

**3.4 Does a bounded head limit worst-case failure?**

Because every RBF unit outputs a value between 0 and 1, the hybrid's prediction cannot move further than ‖**w**~o~‖₁ from its bias (Section 2.4.4). It is tempting to conclude that such a head protects against catastrophic extrapolation. We tested this directly against the otherwise identical network with a linear head (Table 6). The learned ‖**w**~o~‖₁ had a median of [[wo_l1_median:.2f]] (5th–95th percentile [[wo_l1_p5:.2f]]–[[wo_l1_p95:.2f]]; maximum [[wo_l1_max:.1f]]) on the standardised target scale. The bound therefore allows predictions several standard deviations from the training mean, which is wider than the range of most targets. In practice, the worst single-property R² of the two heads was similar: under leave-site-out, [[bd_site_u_worst_rbf:.3f]] for the RBF head and [[bd_site_u_worst_lin:.3f]] for the linear head; under leave-country-out, [[bd_country_u_worst_rbf:.3f]] and [[bd_country_u_worst_lin:.3f]]. The linear head was no worse on the log scale, and the comparison did not depend on clipping to the training range. A bounded output layer therefore gave no practical protection against poor transfer.

Extreme failures had a different cause: the back-transformation of log-modelled properties. Without any range safeguard, [[n_unclipped_lt_m1]] model–property–design combinations had R² below −1, all for total S (skewness 13.9), and they involved GPR, kernel ridge regression, the MLP, ridge regression and SVR. The worst was R² = [[worst_unclipped:.1f]] (ridge regression, total S, random design). In each case a few predictions that were moderately high on the log scale became extreme after exponentiation. Limiting back-transformed predictions to the range of the target in the training fold, a safeguard any deployed system would apply, removed all of these failures. It changed no other result materially, so it was applied identically to every model throughout this paper (Section 2.8). A practical lesson is that skewed soil properties should be evaluated on both scales, and a bounded output layer should not be mistaken for a bounded prediction.

**3.5 Calibration size versus calibration diversity**

A simple comparison of a national calibration with a regional one changes sample size, number of sites and test population at once. Two experiments were designed to separate these factors. In the like-for-like test, the test population was always the three Kenyan sites (Table 7, Figure 7c). Averaged over models, calibrating on the other two Kenyan sites gave a mean pooled R² of [[ken_all_K2:.3f]]. A calibration of exactly the same size drawn from about [[ken_sites_K2pEA_n:.0f]] sites across the region gave [[ken_all_K2pEA_n:.3f]], and was better for [[ken_n_props_div_beats_k2]] of 13 properties. At constant size, then, diversity helped. Using all non-Kenyan samples without any Kenyan sample gave [[ken_all_EA:.3f]], and adding the two other Kenyan sites to them gave the best result, [[ken_all_K2pEA:.3f]] (better than non-Kenyan data alone for [[ken_n_props_full_beats_ea]] of 13 properties). Kenyan samples are therefore valuable, but only within a broad library.

**3.6 Local spiking**

Adding a few samples from the target site improved transfer (Table 8, Figure 8). For kernel ridge regression, mean pooled R² rose from [[sp_KRR_0:.3f]] with no local samples to [[sp_KRR_5:.3f]] with five, [[sp_KRR_20:.3f]] with twenty and [[sp_KRR_50:.3f]] with fifty, and fifty local samples improved all [[sp_KRR_nimp50]] properties for kernel ridge regression, PLSR and LightGBM. The hybrid followed the same shape at a lower level. The largest gains were for properties that transferred only moderately without local data: for kernel ridge regression, total Fe rose from [[sp_KRR_Fe_total_0:.3f]] to [[sp_KRR_Fe_total_50:.3f]], total Cu from [[sp_KRR_Cu_total_0:.3f]] to [[sp_KRR_Cu_total_50:.3f]] and total Mn from [[sp_KRR_Mn_total_0:.3f]] to [[sp_KRR_Mn_total_50:.3f]]. Properties that already transferred well, such as pH ([[sp_KRR_pH_0:.3f]] to [[sp_KRR_pH_50:.3f]]), had less skill to recover. Spiking did not rescue the poorly predicted properties: with fifty local samples, R² for Olsen P reached only [[sp_PLSR_P_Olsen_50:.3f]] with PLSR and [[sp_KRR_P_Olsen_50:.3f]] with kernel ridge regression, and exchangeable K ([[sp_KRR_K_exch_50:.3f]]) and total S ([[sp_KRR_S_total_50:.3f]]) gained little. Local samples therefore correct site-specific bias in calibrations of properties with a usable spectral signal, but for Olsen P they are better used as direct measurements than as spiking samples for a spectral model. This is consistent with earlier spiking studies, in which local samples helped most where a broad calibration was biased for the target area (Guerrero *et al.*, 2010; Wetterlind and Stenberg, 2010).

**3.7 Uncertainty: group-aware conformal prediction**

Prediction intervals that ignore grouping were too narrow under transfer (Table 9, Figure 9). With a random calibration set, nominal 90% intervals covered [[cf_site_random_mean:.1%]] of test samples under leave-site-out, [[cf_aez_random_mean:.1%]] under leave-AEZ-out and [[cf_country_random_mean:.1%]] under leave-country-out, averaged over models. Calibrating on whole held-out sites raised coverage (by property in Table S6) to [[cf_site_group_mean:.1%]], [[cf_aez_group_mean:.1%]] and [[cf_country_group_mean:.1%]]. Group-aware calibration therefore removed about half to two-thirds of the shortfall, but not all of it. The remaining shortfall arises because a withheld country or climatic zone is, on average, further from the training data than a calibration site drawn from within the training region. Wider intervals for samples outside the area of applicability (Meyer and Pebesma, 2021), or calibration weighted by dissimilarity, would be natural extensions. An RBF head does not provide such a signal: when a sample lies far from every centre, its units output values near zero and the network returns a bias-driven prediction without any warning. Conformal intervals give an explicit, calibrated statement of uncertainty instead (Huang *et al.*, 2025; Omondiagbe *et al.*, 2024).

**3.8 Interpretability of attention**

With the selected configurations, each attended position integrates a receptive field of [[rf_cm:.0f]]–[[rf_max_cm:.0f]] cm⁻¹, and adjacent positions are about [[jump_cm:.0f]] cm⁻¹ apart. Attention can therefore indicate broad spectral regions only, and attention peaks should not be read as single wavenumbers. Regions near the atmospheric CO₂ band (2450–2250 cm⁻¹) carry little soil information and were masked before modelling (Section 2.3); the sensitivity analysis showed that masking changed transfer skill by less than 0.01 R² (Table S4).

Figure 10 and Table S5 compare the attention profile with three independent importance measures. Attention agreed closely with integrated gradients (mean Spearman ρ = [[rho_attention_IG:.2f]]), moderately with occlusion (ρ = [[rho_attention_occlusion:.2f]]) and weakly with PLSR VIP (ρ = [[rho_attention_VIP:.2f]]). Occlusion and VIP themselves agreed poorly (ρ = [[rho_occlusion_VIP:.2f]]), so different models rely on different parts of the spectrum to reach similar accuracy. The agreement with integrated gradients partly reflects that both are computed from the same network. The agreement with occlusion is the more informative check, and it is only moderate.

(b, c) Normalised importance profiles for organic C and pH from attention (spread over each position's receptive field), occlusion, integrated gradients (IG) and PLSR VIP, averaged over the eight leave-site-out models. Grey bands mark literature absorbance windows for organic matter (b) and carbonates (c).

For organic C, total N and pH, attention placed more weight inside literature absorbance windows than expected from randomly shifted profiles. The weight was [[ov_OrgC_pct_attention_ratio:.1f]], [[ov_TotN_pct_attention_ratio:.1f]] and [[ov_pH_attention_ratio:.1f]] times the null expectation, with combined permutation p-values of [[ov_OrgC_pct_attention_fisher:.3f]], [[ov_TotN_pct_attention_fisher:.3f]] and [[ov_pH_attention_fisher:.3f]] across the eight folds. Occlusion showed the same enrichment more strongly ([[ov_OrgC_pct_occlusion_ratio:.1f]], [[ov_TotN_pct_occlusion_ratio:.1f]] and [[ov_pH_occlusion_ratio:.1f]] times). For organic C and total N the weight concentrated between about 1650 and 1350 cm⁻¹, the region of carboxylate, amide and aliphatic C–H bending absorbance (Nguyen *et al.*, 1991; Parikh *et al.*, 2014). For pH it concentrated near the carbonate band at about 1450 cm⁻¹ and in the 3250–3000 cm⁻¹ region of O–H and N–H stretching. These patterns are chemically plausible, but they are broad, and few of the individual-fold tests reached significance on their own. We therefore treat attention as a coarse, hypothesis-generating summary, consistent with Jain and Wallace (2019) and Wiegreffe and Pinter (2019), and not as an explanation of individual predictions.

The modality gate was not used as a measure of contribution. Permutation importance showed that depth contributed little for organic C and pH (increase in standardised MSE of [[perm_depth_OrgC_pct:.3f]] and [[perm_depth_pH:.3f]], against [[perm_spec_OrgC_pct:.2f]] and [[perm_spec_pH:.2f]] when the spectra were permuted). For Olsen P and exchangeable K, depth was nearly as informative as the spectra ([[perm_depth_P_Olsen:.3f]] against [[perm_spec_P_Olsen:.3f]]; [[perm_depth_K_exch:.3f]] against [[perm_spec_K_exch:.3f]]). This agrees with the ablation, in which removing depth produced significant losses for Olsen P and exchangeable K (and for total N, S and Fe). The gate weights (spectral share [[gate_min:.2f]]–[[gate_max:.2f]] across properties) loosely tracked the permutation shares (ρ = [[gate_rho_spec:.2f]]). Because gate weights are not identifiable (the encoders can rescale their outputs to compensate), they should not be read as percentage contributions of each input.

**3.9 Agronomic screening in unsampled landscapes**

Deficiency was not assessed for total Cu, total Zn, quasi-total S or total N. Aqua regia and total determinations do not measure plant-available fractions, and deficiency diagnosis for Cu and Zn requires an extraction such as DTPA (Lindsay and Norvell, 1978). Because the samples are not a probability sample, no population prevalence is estimated for any property either. We ask a narrower and more useful question: can a spectral model, applied in an unsampled landscape, correctly flag samples below an indicative critical level? Screening was reliable for organic C (balanced accuracy [[scr_OrgC_pct_bal_acc:.2f]]), pH below 5.5 ([[scr_pH_bal_acc:.2f]]) and low eCEC ([[scr_eCEC_bal_acc:.2f]]), and moderate for exchangeable Ca and Mg. It was weak for exchangeable K ([[scr_K_exch_bal_acc:.2f]]). For Olsen P it was close to chance ([[scr_P_Olsen_bal_acc:.2f]]): with [[scr_P_Olsen_prevalence:.0%]] of samples below 15 mg/kg, a model that predicts low values almost everywhere achieves high sensitivity ([[scr_P_Olsen_sens:.2f]]) but cannot identify the samples that are adequate (specificity [[scr_P_Olsen_spec:.2f]]). Phosphorus is the nutrient on which fertiliser decisions most often turn in these systems, so this is the most important practical limitation of the library.

The failure for Olsen P is unlikely to be caused by reference-method precision alone. Even if the reference values carried a 30% relative error plus a 1 mg/kg absolute error, the attainable R² would still be about [[pceil_30_1.0_orig:.2f]] on the original scale and [[pceil_30_1.0_log:.2f]] on the log scale, far above what any model achieved. Two other explanations remain plausible. Olsen P measures a small, labile pool that has weak direct MIR expression and depends on management history; and the interval between scanning (2009–2013) and chemical analysis (2017–2018) may have changed extractable P in storage (Bartlett and James, 1980). The present data cannot separate these explanations. Paired scanning and analysis of fresh samples would resolve the question.

**3.10 Comparison with published results**

Published accuracies are rarely accompanied by a statement of validation design, and Section 3.1 shows that the design alone shifts mean R² by about 0.3. Hengl *et al.* (2017) mapped African soil nutrients from environmental covariates and identified P, S and B among the poorest-predicted properties, as found here from spectra. Gruszczyński and Gruszczyński (2022), using a random split of the same AfSIS archive, reported R² of 0.02–0.25 for Mehlich-3 P across three algorithms, consistent with our results for Olsen P. Ng *et al.* (2019) and Shen and Viscarra Rossel (2021) reported clear gains from CNNs with libraries of tens of thousands of spectra. With 1,310 samples, we found the plain CNN to be competitive but not superior. Our results do not contradict theirs, but they suggest that architectural gains reported on large single-region libraries should not be assumed to carry over to a small, multi-country library evaluated by spatial transfer. Attention-based designs reported recently (Guo *et al.*, 2025; Shuai *et al.*, 2025; Yin *et al.*, 2026) were evaluated within single regions; whether their gains survive spatial transfer remains to be tested.

**3.11 Practical implications**

Four recommendations follow, in order of priority. **Broaden the library before refining the model.** At constant size, more sites gave better transfer; and 20–50 local samples improved transfer to a new landscape for every property, though only marginally for Olsen P, exchangeable K and total S. **Report the validation design with every accuracy figure,** and use group-aware intervals, since random calibration under-covered by 10–20 percentage points. **Choose among established learners on convenience,** because GPR, kernel methods, MBL, gradient boosting, PLSR and a plain CNN were statistically close. GPR is a reasonable default for libraries of this size because it also provides predictive variances. **Do not rely on spectra for Olsen P;** use direct measurement, and use the spectral model to stratify where measurements should be taken.

**3.12 Limitations**

\(i) Agro-ecological zones were approximated by Köppen–Geiger climate classes, and the arid (BWh) and monsoon (Am) groups each contain one site. (ii) Three Tanzanian sites contribute only six samples, and Uganda contributes two sites, so country-level results are indicative. (iii) All spectra come from one instrument in one laboratory, so instrument transfer (Gamagedara *et al.*, 2025) was not tested. (iv) The ancillary branch carries only sampling depth; satellite, terrain and climate covariates were not used, because coordinates and covariates derived from them carry the extrapolation risk shown in Section 3.3. (v) About 3,300 unlabelled spectra from the same countries were not used. Self-supervised pretraining on them is a natural next step for the neural models. (vi) Neural search spaces were small (three configurations per model) because every model was trained from five initialisations inside every fold. A larger search could improve the neural models, but it would not change the conclusion that their gains over conventional learners are small. (vii) The time gap between scanning and chemical analysis, and the absence of replicate precision data, limit the interpretation of the P and K results.
